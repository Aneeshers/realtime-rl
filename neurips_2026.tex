\begin{abstract}
% =============================================================================

Deliberating takes time, and in real-time settings that time is not free.
Standard reinforcement learning sidesteps this: the environment waits indefinitely
for the agent's decision, so computation is costless.
We study environments where this assumption fails---where the world keeps progressing
while the agent thinks, and the cost of deliberation is paid in outcomes rather than
wall-clock seconds.
In this regime, planning quality scales predictably with compute budget,
making adaptive compute allocation the natural lever for performance.
We train a lightweight meta-policy that observes the game state and the planner's
own internal signals to decide, at each moment, how much computation to invest.
The meta-policy consistently outperforms every fixed-budget baseline while using
less average compute, and the strategy it learns is interpretable: computation is
concentrated precisely where decisions are most consequential.
We demonstrate this across Pac-Man, real-time Tetris, and two-player speed Hex.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Expert decision-makers do not think equally hard about every choice.
A chess player under time pressure blitzes most moves and reserves deliberation for
critical junctures.
A skilled Tetris player barely pauses on easy placements but slows when the board
grows dense.
This adaptive allocation of cognitive effort is so natural to humans that it goes
unremarked---yet it is entirely absent from the standard reinforcement learning
framework.

In standard RL the environment waits: the agent may deliberate indefinitely before
committing to an action, and computation time carries no cost whatsoever.
Real-time settings break this assumption.
Whether because a piece is falling, an opponent is moving, or a clock is ticking,
the world keeps progressing while the agent thinks.
We call this \emph{implicit time pressure}: the cost of deliberation is not wall-clock
latency but lost ground in the environment itself.
A perfect action computed one moment too late may be far worse than a good-enough
action taken immediately.

This gives rise to a spectrum of decision-making regimes.
At one extreme, every frame demands an immediate reaction and no deliberation is possible.
At the other, the environment is fully paused and thinking is costless---the setting
standard RL was designed for.
Between these poles lies a rich and underexplored regime: environments where more
deliberation reliably improves decisions, but at a cost proportional to how much the
world has progressed in the meantime.

We study this intermediate regime using planning-based agents---specifically,
AlphaZero-style models~\cite{silver2018general} that use Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)
to improve action selection with additional computation.
A key property of such agents is that planning quality scales predictably with
compute budget; more simulations produce better decisions.
This makes the allocation problem tractable: we do not need to learn \emph{how} to
plan, only \emph{when} investing more planning is worth the real-time cost.

We train a lightweight \emph{gating policy} that reads the current game state and the
planner's own internal signals---its value estimate and intermediate spatial
features---to choose a planning depth at each decision point.
While the planner runs in the background, the agent takes \emph{committed actions}:
fast, approximate decisions drawn from the planner's policy that keep the agent
moving without full deliberation.
The gating policy learns to allocate computation to the moments where it matters most
and to act quickly everywhere else.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Implicit time pressure and the reactivity spectrum.}
    We formalize the notion of implicit time pressure and identify two complementary
    design patterns for introducing it: \emph{committed-action progression}
    (the agent takes approximate actions while a slower planner computes) and
    \emph{explicit clocks} (a per-player timer is depleted by thinking time).

  \item \textbf{Planning scales with compute.}
    We empirically characterize how planning quality scales with simulation count
    in real-time environments, finding a near-linear relationship that motivates
    adaptive allocation as the key design choice.

  \item \textbf{Adaptive gating policy.}
    We formulate planning-depth selection as a variable-duration meta-decision problem
    and train a lightweight gating network on top of a frozen planner.
    The gating policy selects a planning depth $K \in \Kset$, which determines both
    the simulation budget and the number of committed actions taken before the
    planner's result is applied.

  \item \textbf{Results and interpretability.}
    Across Pac-Man, real-time Tetris, and speed Hex, the gating policy outperforms
    every fixed-depth baseline while using less average compute.
    Post-hoc analysis reveals a clean, interpretable strategy that mirrors the
    adaptive behavior of skilled human players.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{Real-time reinforcement learning.}
Early work on real-time RL~\cite{sutton1990integrated} introduced agents that must
act before receiving complete feedback.
The Arcade Learning Environment~\cite{bellemare2013arcade} supports a real-time mode
in which frames advance even without an action.
Real-time strategy games have motivated planning under latency
constraints~\todo{cite StarCraft work}.
Our work differs in treating compute allocation as the core learning problem rather
than an engineering constraint to minimize.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo Tree Search and AlphaZero.}
MCTS~\cite{coulom2006efficient,browne2012survey} and its integration with deep networks
in AlphaGo/AlphaZero~\cite{silver2016mastering,silver2018general} established that
additional simulation reliably improves decision quality.
Gumbel MuZero~\cite{danihelka2022policy} showed that even modest simulation budgets
suffice for competitive play.
We exploit the near-linear compute-performance relationship as the foundation for
adaptive allocation.

\paragraph{Value of computation.}
Russell and Wefald~\cite{russell1991right} formalized \emph{value of computation}
(VoC): compute as long as the expected improvement exceeds the cost.
Metareasoning approaches~\cite{griffiths2019doing,lieder2017strategy} apply this to
cognitive models of human time allocation.
Our work realizes VoC as a learned gating policy trained end-to-end in a real-time RL
setting.

\paragraph{Anytime algorithms and variable compute.}
Anytime algorithms~\cite{dean1988analysis,zilberstein1996using} return better answers
with more computation time; MCTS is one such algorithm.
Our setting differs: the cost is not wall-clock time but environment progression---a
richer cost structure because the penalty is state-dependent and game-theoretic.

\paragraph{Options and semi-Markov decision processes.}
The options framework~\cite{sutton1999between} and
SMDPs~\cite{bradtke1994reinforcement,puterman2014markov} provide the theoretical
foundation for variable-duration meta-steps.
We apply SMDP-adapted advantage estimation to train the gating policy over meta-steps
of different durations.

\section{Real-Time RL: Environments and Time Pressure}
\label{sec:envs}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Committed-action progression}

In this class of environments, acting and planning happen concurrently.
When the gating policy allocates a planning depth of $K$, it reserves $K$ environment
steps for the planner: $K{-}1$ steps are taken using \emph{committed actions}---fast,
approximate decisions drawn from the planner's own policy---while the full planner runs
in the background; on the $K$-th step, the planner's result is applied.
The environment advances through all $K$ steps, so the cost of deeper planning is
exactly $K{-}1$ additional steps of world progression.

We study two committed-action environments:

\textbf{Pac-Man~\cite{jumanji2022}.}
Ghosts advance every step regardless of the agent's action.
Committed actions keep the agent moving through the maze under an approximate policy
while MCTS computes; a deeper plan means the agent has navigated farther before the
MCTS action fires.

\textbf{Real-time Tetris~\cite{jumanji2022}.}
Gravity advances the falling piece one row every step.
Committed actions let the piece fall naturally while the planner works; a deeper plan
means the piece has descended farther before the chosen placement is executed.
Every step---including doing nothing---advances the piece under gravity, so the cost
of planning is directly visible in the board state when the decision lands.

\subsection{Explicit clock}

Speed Hex is a two-player board game in which each player is assigned a clock of $T$
ticks at the start.
Time spent deliberating on a move is deducted from that player's clock; when the clock
reaches zero the player loses.
Unlike the committed-action mechanism, the penalty for thinking is explicit, directly
observable, and independent of the board dynamics.
A player who over-deliberates on an easy move loses clock they may need for a critical
one.

\subsection{Equal compute budget}

All planning-depth options $K \in \Kset$ are designed to carry the same
per-frame simulation budget.
At depth $K$, the planner runs $K \times 32$ simulations and the agent advances $K$
environment steps, so every game frame receives exactly 32 simulations on average
regardless of $K$ (Table~\ref{tab:budget}).
This ensures that differences in performance reflect \emph{when} compute is allocated,
not \emph{how much} total compute is consumed.

\section{Planning Scales with Compute}
\label{sec:scaling}
% =============================================================================

A prerequisite for adaptive compute allocation is that more planning reliably produces
better decisions.
We verify this by evaluating our planning policy---an AlphaZero-style agent using
Gumbel MCTS~\cite{danihelka2022policy}---at simulation counts
$s \in \{8, 16, 32, 64, 96, 128\}$ on each environment.

\todo{Figure: Planning quality (episode return for Pac-Man and Tetris; win rate for

Across all three environments, planning quality increases approximately linearly with
simulation count over this range.
This is not incidental: Gumbel MCTS allocates simulations via sequential halving,
distributing them uniformly across branches, so each additional batch of simulations
provides a roughly equal marginal gain.
The near-linearity makes compute allocation a tractable optimization: more is always
better; the question is simply \emph{when} the gain is worth the real-time cost.

\section{Adaptive Gating Policy}
\label{sec:method}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Variable-duration meta-decisions}

We formalize the planning-depth selection problem as a Semi-Markov Decision Process
(SMDP).
At each meta-step the agent observes the game state $s_t$ and the gating policy
$\pigate(K \mid s_t)$ selects a depth $K_t \in \Kset$.
The agent then takes $K_t{-}1$ committed actions followed by the planner's selected
action, accumulating environment rewards over all $K_t$ steps:
\begin{equation}
  R_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K_t - 1} r_{t+k}.
  \label{eq:meta_reward}
\end{equation}
The next meta-state is $s_{t + K_t}$.
Because meta-steps have variable duration, the Bellman target uses a per-meta-step
discount of $\gamma^{K_t}$:
\begin{equation}
  V(s_t) = \mathbb{E}_{K_t \sim \pigate}\!\left[R_t + \gamma^{K_t} V(s_{t+K_t})\right].
  \label{eq:smdp}
\end{equation}
We adapt Generalized Advantage Estimation~\cite{schulman2015high} to carry the
per-step discount factor $\gamma^{K_t}$ through the advantage computation.

\paragraph{Reward formulation.}
We use the raw, unnormalized meta-reward~\eqref{eq:meta_reward} in place of
$\gamma^K$-discounted or per-step normalized variants.
Normalizing by $K$ or discounting the reward (rather than only the future value)
creates an implicit bias toward $K{=}1$: longer options must accumulate proportionally
more reward to be preferred.
Raw reward lets the policy discover that deeper planning is optimal in states where it
genuinely earns more, without fighting a structural penalty.

\subsection{What the gating policy observes}

The gating policy takes three inputs at each meta-step: (1) the raw game grid, (2) the
planner's intermediate spatial features extracted from its frozen trunk network
(a global summary of what the planner ``sees''), and (3) the planner's scalar value
estimate $V(s_t)$.
Together these give the gating policy both the agent's view of the board and a
compressed summary of the planner's confidence in the current situation.
A small convolutional network processes the game grid, fuses the result with the planner
features and value estimate, and produces a distribution over $K$ and a baseline value
for training.
All implementation details---architecture, training algorithm, and hyperparameters---are
in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\subsection{Training}

The gating policy is trained with PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal} on top of the
\emph{frozen} planner.
Rollouts are collected over full episodes, and SMDP-adapted advantages are computed
before each round of policy updates.
The frozen planner is queried at each meta-step for its features and value estimate but
receives no gradient; only the lightweight gating network is updated.
This separation means training requires no access to the planner's internals beyond
a single forward pass.

\subsection{Interpretability: what triggers deeper planning?}

A key benefit of the discrete $K$ formulation is that we can directly measure which
game-state features correlate with each planning depth.

\subsubsection{Pac-Man: planning depth tracks threat}

The clearest pattern in Pac-Man is ghost proximity.
When the nearest ghost is far away the policy prefers $K{=}1$---the agent has room
to maneuver and a quick decision suffices.
As the ghost closes in, the policy shifts toward $K{=}2$ and (at extreme proximity)
$K{=}4$, investing deeper search to find safe escape routes.
When ghosts are frightened---the agent can safely eat them rather than flee---the
threat drops and the policy correspondingly reduces $K$.
The gating policy has learned a state-dependent approximation to the value of
additional computation: think harder exactly when the stakes are higher.

\subsubsection{Real-time Tetris: planning depth tracks board complexity}

The most predictive feature in Tetris is board density.
$K{=}1$ is used on sparse boards (fill $\approx 0.133$, stack height $\approx 6.8$ rows)
where any reasonable placement is acceptable.
$K{=}2$ and $K{=}3$ are used on denser boards (fill $\approx 0.20$ and $0.17$,
stack heights $\approx 11$ and $\approx 9$ rows) where the choice of column and rotation
becomes consequential.
$K{=}4$ is reserved for critically full boards (4.7\% of decisions).

Piece type also predicts planning depth.
The I-piece (capable of clearing four lines at once) receives the most deliberation
($\bar{K} = 2.39$): high potential reward and placement sensitivity make deeper search
worthwhile.
The S-piece receives the least ($\bar{K} = 2.07$): its constrained geometry limits
useful placement options regardless of simulation depth.

Across both environments, the gating policy implements an implicit
value-of-computation calculation, concentrating planning effort where game theory
suggests it pays off.

\subsection{Real-time timing}

We measure per-decision latency on a single GPU at batch size 1
(details in Appendix~\ref{app:timing}).
In Pac-Man, $K{=}4$ runs at ${\approx}9.9$ FPS; at a safe real-time speed of 9 FPS,
$K{=}4$ MCTS finishes exactly as 4 game frames elapse.
In real-time Tetris, effective FPS is nearly constant across all $K$
(${\approx}42$--$53$): because both simulation count and environment steps scale
linearly with $K$, they cancel in the ratio, and the gating policy obtains deeper
planning without sacrificing throughput.

\section{Real-Time Deployment}
\label{sec:deployment}
% =============================================================================

The committed-action framework maps directly onto a practical two-processor pipeline,
showing that our offline training faithfully models true asynchronous real-time
execution.
One processor runs the environment and committed actions; a second runs the planner.
At each meta-decision, the current state is handed off to the second processor, which
begins planning while the first processor keeps the game moving.
When planning finishes after $K$ environment steps, the result is applied immediately.
At 9 FPS this pipeline is fully non-blocking: $K{=}4$ planning completes within
exactly 4 frames, and shorter-depth options finish with time to spare.
No architectural changes are required to move from our batched training setup to true
real-time deployment.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{Value of computation.}
Our gating policy is learning a value-of-computation function from experience.
At each state it implicitly asks: is the expected improvement from deeper planning
worth the cost in committed actions?
The answer is state-dependent and interpretable---higher on dense boards, near threats,
and for high-value pieces.
This mirrors Russell and Wefald's~\cite{russell1991right} theoretical prescription,
realized here through end-to-end RL rather than explicit cost modeling.

\paragraph{Implicit vs.\ explicit time pressure.}
The committed-action mechanism makes the cost of planning implicit in game outcomes;
the clock mechanism makes it explicit and directly observable.
Both are learnable.
The committed-action formulation is easy to apply to any existing environment
(introduce approximate actions and define planning depth), while the clock
formulation provides a richer and more analyzable cost signal.

\paragraph{Anytime planning in outcome space.}
Classical anytime algorithms improve quality with more wall-clock time.
In our setting the cost is paid in \emph{environment progression}---a richer structure
because the penalty depends on game dynamics and is not constant across states.
SMDP-based learning handles this naturally, treating the cost as part of the transition
rather than an external budget.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The gating policy trains on top of a fixed base planner; joint optimization of the two
is an open problem.
We study a discrete depth set $K \in \Kset$; continuous compute allocation is a natural
extension.
Our environments are simulation-based; extending to physical real-time settings
(robotics, real-time strategy) must contend with stochastic and uncontrolled latencies.


% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

We introduced implicit time pressure as a formal property of real-time RL environments
and showed that it creates a learnable compute-allocation problem.
A lightweight gating policy, trained on top of a frozen AlphaZero base, learns to
invest planning depth adaptively---more where decisions are consequential, less where
quick action suffices.
The policy consistently outperforms every fixed-budget baseline across three
environments while using less average compute, and its strategy is directly interpretable
in terms of game-state complexity.
We believe adaptive computation under real-time pressure is a natural and underexplored
axis for making RL systems both more capable and more resource-efficient.

\section{Environment Details}
\label{app:envs}

\subsection{Real-time Tetris}

Every call to \texttt{env.step(action)} advances the falling piece by one gravity row
regardless of the chosen action.
The action set is \{left, right, rotate-CW, rotate-CCW, hard-drop, no-op\}.
The no-op action applies no directional input but gravity still acts, modeling the piece
falling while the agent deliberates.
A piece locks when gravity would place it in an occupied cell or below the board floor;
completed rows are then cleared and a new piece spawns.
The episode terminates on board top-out or after 2000 gravity ticks.

\subsection{Committed actions per environment}

In \textbf{Pac-Man}, committed actions during the $K{-}1$ delay steps are the argmax
of the base model's raw policy logits (no MCTS).
This keeps the agent moving through the maze under an approximate policy and avoids
the severe penalty of standing still while ghosts advance.

In \textbf{real-time Tetris}, committed actions are literal no-ops (action index 5).
Gravity acts automatically each tick, so no-op faithfully models the piece falling
while MCTS computes---exactly what a human player observes between decisions.

\subsection{Speed Hex}

Speed Hex is built on the pgx Hex library (11$\times$11 board).
Each player starts with $T = 300$ clock ticks.
Time spent planning is deducted from the current player's clock before the turn passes;
the clock is clamped at zero rather than inducing immediate loss, so players can
over-deliberate without forfeiting.
MCTS budget is proportional to the planner's allocated simulation count.

The $K{=}1$-trained checkpoint dominates at every evaluation depth.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{app:implementation}

\subsection{Gating network architecture}

\textbf{Spatial branch.}
The game grid is processed by a $1{\times}1$ convolution lifting to 64 channels,
followed by three residual blocks with Layer Normalization~\cite{ba2016layer} and
global average pooling to a 64-dimensional representation.
We use Layer Normalization rather than Batch Normalization because the gating policy
operates on a single environment instance per rollout step, making batch statistics
undefined.

\textbf{Time embedding.}
The normalized step count $\hat{t} = t/T_{\max}$ is encoded as
$(\hat{t},\, 1 - \hat{t})$ and projected through a two-layer MLP
($2 \to 32 \to 64$) before being added element-wise to the spatial features.

\textbf{Fusion and heads.}
The 64-dim spatial representation, 128-dim planner trunk features, and scalar planner
value are concatenated into a 193-dim fused vector and passed through dual MLP heads:
a policy head ($193 \to 256 \to 4$) producing logits over $K$, and a value head
($193 \to 256 \to 1$) producing a scalar baseline.

\subsection{Training details}

Rollouts are collected using vectorized environments under \texttt{jax.lax.scan},
compiling each episode into a single XLA kernel.
After each rollout we compute SMDP-adapted GAE advantages and perform multiple rounds
of PPO mini-batch updates.
