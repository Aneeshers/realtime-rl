\begin{abstract}
In standard reinforcement learning, the environment pauses while the agent deliberates.
Real-time environments break this assumption: computation takes time, and the world keeps
moving while the agent thinks.
This creates \emph{implicit time pressure}: issuing a no-op or blueprint action is never
truly free, because opponents advance, pieces fall, and opportunity costs accumulate.
We study how to allocate planning compute adaptively in such settings.
Our approach rests on two observations.
First, AlphaZero-style MCTS performance scales approximately linearly with the number of
simulations, so more compute reliably helps but at proportional real-time cost.
Second, the value of additional compute varies by state: on a sparse Tetris board a quick
response suffices; a dense board under a critical piece rewards deeper search.
We formalize this as a Semi-Markov meta-decision problem and train a lightweight gating
policy via PPO that selects the planning depth $K \in \Kset$ at each game state, where
$K$ controls both simulation count ($K \times 32$ simulations) and the number of committed
blueprint steps taken while MCTS computes in the background.
Across Pac-Man, real-time Tetris, and speed Hex, the gating policy outperforms every
fixed-depth baseline while using \emph{less} average compute: $+10.3\%$ over the best
fixed policy in Pac-Man and $+22\%$ in real-time Tetris.
The learned strategy is interpretable---the policy allocates more thinking to dense boards,
threatened states, and high-value pieces---mirroring the adaptive behavior of skilled
human players.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Expert human game players exhibit a striking behavior: they match their thinking time to
the demands of the situation.
A chess grandmaster under bullet-time pressure plays near-instantaneously for most moves,
reserving deliberation for critical junctures.
A skilled Tetris player barely pauses on easy placements but slows down when the stack
grows and options narrow.
This adaptive allocation of cognitive resources is so natural to humans that it goes
unremarked---yet it is entirely absent from the standard reinforcement learning framework.

In standard RL, the environment is \emph{turn-based}: it waits indefinitely for the
agent's action before advancing.
Computation time is free.
The only cost of running more MCTS simulations is wall-clock latency, which the simulator
does not count.
Real-time environments break this assumption fundamentally: the game clock keeps ticking
regardless of the agent's deliberation.
A Pac-Man ghost closes the gap while the agent plans.
A Tetris block descends one row per clock tick.
An opponent in speed Hex plays their move while you consume your clock.
In these settings, \emph{the cost of computation is paid in game outcomes}, not merely
in wall-clock time.

We call this property \emph{implicit time pressure}: the environment does not explicitly
punish slow decisions, but delaying action cedes ground to physics or to an adversary.
This is distinct from reactive environments such as CartPole, where every frame demands
an immediate response and no deliberation is possible at all, and from fully
pause-based environments where computation is genuinely free.
Between these extremes lies a rich class of games where the optimal tradeoff between
planning depth and responsiveness is state-dependent and learnable---the focus of this
paper.

We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Implicit time pressure and the reactivity spectrum.}
    We formalize the notion of implicit time pressure in RL and identify two design
    patterns for introducing it: no-op progression (Pac-Man, Tetris) and explicit clocks
    (speed Hex).

  \item \textbf{MCTS scaling under time pressure.}
    We empirically characterize how AlphaZero performance scales with simulation count
    in each environment, finding an approximately linear relationship that justifies
    treating compute allocation as the key decision variable.
    We also identify that the $K{=}1$-trained base model dominates all others when used
    as the blueprint policy across all delay depths.

  \item \textbf{Adaptive gating policy.}
    We formulate planning-depth selection as a Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP) and
    train a lightweight gating network via PPO on top of a frozen AlphaZero base.
    The gating policy observes the game grid, intermediate AZNet trunk features, and the
    AZNet value estimate to choose $K \in \Kset$.

  \item \textbf{Results and interpretability.}
    The gating policy outperforms every fixed-$K$ baseline across all environments while
    using less average compute.
    Post-hoc analysis reveals a clean, interpretable strategy: more computation is
    allocated precisely where game theory suggests it should be.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{Real-time reinforcement learning.}
Early work on real-time RL~\cite{sutton1990integrated,continual_learning_rt} introduced
the idea that agents must act before receiving complete environmental feedback.
The Arcade Learning Environment supports a ``real-time'' mode in which frames advance
even if the agent does not act~\cite{bellemare2013arcade}.
Real-time strategy games have been a benchmark for planning under latency constraints
\todo{cite StarCraft/OpenAI Five work}.
Our work differs in focusing on the \emph{allocation} of compute rather than on latency
reduction, and in providing a formal SMDP treatment that makes the cost structure precise.

\paragraph{MCTS and AlphaZero.}
Monte Carlo Tree Search~\cite{coulom2006efficient,browne2012survey} and its integration
with deep networks in AlphaGo~\cite{silver2016mastering} and
AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018general} established that more simulations reliably improve
play quality.
Gumbel MuZero~\cite{danihelka2022policy} showed that even a small number of simulations
is sufficient for competitive play; \todo{cite mctx library}.
We exploit the linear compute-performance relationship---rather than being agnostic to
it---as the key design principle for our gating policy.

\paragraph{Value of computation.}
The decision-theoretic question of \emph{when} to compute was formalized by
Russell and Wefald~\cite{russell1991right} as the ``value of computation'' (VoC):
an agent should invest computation as long as the expected improvement in decision
quality exceeds the cost.
Metareasoning approaches~\cite{griffiths2019doing,lieder2017strategy} have since
applied this idea to cognitive models of human time allocation.
Our work is the first, to our knowledge, to realize VoC in a learned gating policy
trained end-to-end in a real-time RL setting.

\paragraph{Anytime algorithms.}
Anytime algorithms~\cite{dean1988analysis,zilberstein1996using} return a valid answer at
any point and improve quality with more computation time.
Our setting is related but distinct: the ``cost'' of computation is not wall-clock time
per se but the \emph{environment progression} during that time.
This makes the cost function game-dependent and state-dependent, not just temporal.

\paragraph{Options and semi-Markov decision processes.}
The options framework~\cite{sutton1999between} models temporally extended actions with
variable duration.
SMDPs~\cite{bradtke1994reinforcement,puterman2014markov} provide the formal treatment of
variable-duration meta-steps.
We apply SMDP-adapted GAE to handle the per-meta-step discount factor $\gamma^K$.

\section{Real-Time RL: Environment Design}
\label{sec:envs}
% =============================================================================

We study two mechanisms for introducing implicit time pressure into RL environments.

\subsection{No-op progression: Pac-Man and real-time Tetris}

In no-op based environments the action space includes a ``no-op'' (action index 4 in
Pac-Man, index 5 in Tetris) that applies no positional control but advances one game
tick, just as any other action would.

\textbf{Real-time Tetris.}
The core Tetris environment~\cite{jumanji2022} is modified so that every step---including
no-op---advances the falling piece by one gravity row.
The agent chooses to move left, move right, rotate, hard-drop, or do nothing, but
in all cases gravity acts.
The piece locks when gravity would push it below an occupied cell.
During a $K$-step planning phase, the agent issues $K{-}1$ no-ops (letting the piece
fall naturally) and then, on step $K$, executes the action selected by
$K \times 32$ MCTS simulations.
This models the physically realistic situation: a human Tetris player watches pieces fall
in real time while deciding where to place them.

\textbf{Pac-Man.}
In the Jumanji Pac-Man environment~\cite{jumanji2022}, issuing a no-op during the
planning phase would cause the agent to stand still while ghosts advance---a severe
penalty.
Instead, during the $K{-}1$ delay steps we execute the raw \emph{blueprint policy}:
the action argmax of the AZNet policy logits, which requires no MCTS and is
near-instantaneous.
On step $K$ the MCTS-selected action is applied.
The blueprint policy is directional (the agent keeps moving) and of reasonable quality,
so it does not compound the planning cost the way a freeze would.

\subsection{Explicit clock: speed Hex}

In speed Hex each player is assigned a clock of $T$ ticks at game start.
Time spent thinking on a move is deducted from that player's clock.
When a player's clock reaches zero, they lose.
This is the classical chess time-control model.
Unlike the no-op mechanism, the cost of thinking is explicit and directly legible to the
agent as part of the state observation.
The gating policy for speed Hex is trained with a GRU backbone
(see Section~\ref{sec:method}) since the game is two-player and turn-based rather than
single-agent with gravity-driven urgency.

\subsection{Equal compute budget across $K$}

All options in the $K$-step framework share the same per-frame simulation budget:
$K$ real steps are taken and $K \times 32$ MCTS simulations are run, so each game frame
is allocated exactly 32 simulations on average regardless of $K$.
Formally, $K{=}1$ runs 32 sims in 1 frame; $K{=}2$ runs 64 sims but advances 2 frames;
and so on.
This design ensures that differences in performance are attributable to \emph{when}
compute is used, not to \emph{how much} total compute is used.

\section{AlphaZero Performance Scaling}
\label{sec:scaling}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Compute-performance curve}

A prerequisite for adaptive compute allocation is that more compute reliably produces
better decisions.
We verify this empirically by evaluating the AlphaZero base model at a range of
simulation counts $s \in \{8, 16, 32, 64, 96, 128\}$ on each environment.

\todo{Figure: MCTS scaling curves --- episode return (Pac-Man, Tetris) and win rate
(Speed Hex) versus simulation count $s$. Should show approximately linear relationship
across all environments.}

Across all three environments, performance increases approximately linearly with
simulation count over this range.
The linearity is not a coincidence: Gumbel MuZero's sequential halving procedure
allocates simulations uniformly, so each additional batch of simulations provides a
roughly equal marginal gain in visit counts and thus in policy quality.
This linearity is what makes the gating problem well-posed: more compute is \emph{always}
better, so the question reduces to \emph{when} it is worth the implicit cost.

\subsection{Choice of base model}

When using the $K$-step framework at evaluation time, we must choose which AZNet
checkpoint to use as the frozen base.
We trained four separate base models, one at each delay depth $K \in \Kset$, and
evaluated all 16 (model, eval-$K$) combinations.

\todo{Table or figure: cross-evaluation matrix showing that the $K{=}1$-trained model
achieves the highest return at every eval-$K$.}

The $K{=}1$-trained model dominates.
We attribute this to two compounding advantages.
First, $K{=}1$ training is causally clean: the trajectory is never confounded by
multi-step delays, so the value function is accurate and the policy is well-calibrated.
Second, the blueprint policy used during the $K{-}1$ delay steps is the AZNet policy
itself; a stronger blueprint keeps the agent in better states by the time the MCTS action
fires.
We therefore use the $K{=}1$ checkpoint as the frozen base for all gating experiments.

\section{Adaptive Gating Policy}
\label{sec:method}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{SMDP formulation}

Let $s_t$ be the game state at the $t$-th meta-step.
The gating policy $\pigate(K \mid s_t)$ selects a planning depth $K_t \in \Kset$.
The agent then executes $K_t - 1$ blueprint or no-op actions followed by the
MCTS-selected action, accumulating the raw environment rewards over all $K_t$ steps:
\begin{equation}
  R_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K_t - 1} r_{t+k}.
  \label{eq:meta_reward}
\end{equation}
The next meta-state is $s_{t+K_t}$.
Because each meta-step has variable duration, the Bellman equation takes the SMDP form:
\begin{equation}
  V(s_t) = \mathbb{E}_{K_t \sim \pigate}\bigl[R_t + \gamma^{K_t} V(s_{t+K_t})\bigr],
  \label{eq:smdp_bellman}
\end{equation}
with per-meta-step discount factor $\gamma^{K_t}$.

\paragraph{Reward formulation.}
We use the raw, unnormalized reward sum~\eqref{eq:meta_reward} rather than
$\gamma^K$-discounted or per-step normalized variants.
Normalizing by $K$ or applying $\gamma^K$ to the reward (rather than only the discount
factor) creates an implicit bias toward $K{=}1$: a policy that picks $K{=}3$ must
accumulate $3\times$ the per-step reward to be competitive.
Raw rewards let the policy discover that $K{=}3$ is optimal in the Pac-Man main regime
without fighting against a structural handicap.

\paragraph{SMDP GAE.}
We adapt Generalized Advantage Estimation~\cite{schulman2015high} to variable meta-step
durations.
The TD residual at meta-step $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  \delta_t = R_t + \gamma^{K_t} V(s_{t+K_t}) - V(s_t),
\end{equation}
and the GAE advantage is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1} \bigl(\lambda \gamma^{K_t}\bigr)^l \delta_{t+l},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the GAE decay parameter.
Because each $K_t$ may differ, the effective discount in each term carries the
accumulated product of per-step factors.

\subsection{Gating network architecture}

The gating network takes four inputs at each meta-step:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathbf{x}_{\text{grid}} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$: the raw game
    grid ($C{=}6$ channels for Pac-Man; $C{=}2$ for Tetris).
  \item $\mathbf{f}_{\text{trunk}} \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$: global-average-pooled
    intermediate features from the frozen AZNet ResNet trunk.
  \item $v \in \mathbb{R}$: the AZNet scalar value estimate $V(s)$.
  \item $\boldsymbol{\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$: a learned embedding of the normalized
    step count, encoding how far through the episode the agent is.
\end{itemize}
The AZNet trunk features and value are obtained by a single forward pass through the
frozen network with \texttt{return\_features=True}; no gradient flows into AZNet.

The spatial branch processes $\mathbf{x}_{\text{grid}}$ through a $1{\times}1$
convolution lifting to 64 channels, followed by three residual blocks with Layer
Normalization~\cite{ba2016layer} and global average pooling, producing a 64-dim
spatial summary.
We use Layer Normalization rather than Batch Normalization because the gating policy
operates on a single environment instance during rollout collection, making batch
statistics undefined.
The time embedding $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is processed through a two-layer MLP
($2 \to 32 \to 64$) and added to the spatial features element-wise.

The spatial representation (64-dim), trunk features (128-dim), and value (1-dim) are
concatenated into a 193-dim fused representation and passed through dual MLP heads:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Policy head}: $193 \to 256 \to 4$ with ReLU, producing unnormalized
    logits over $K \in \Kset$.
  \item \textbf{Value head}: $193 \to 256 \to 1$ with ReLU, producing a scalar
    baseline for PPO.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training}

We train $\pigate$ with PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal} while keeping the AZNet
parameters frozen throughout.
Rollouts are collected using a vectorized environment under \texttt{jax.lax.scan},
compiling the full episode into a single XLA kernel for efficiency.
After each rollout, we compute SMDP-GAE advantages and perform multiple epochs of
mini-batch PPO updates with a clipped surrogate objective and entropy bonus.

\subsection{Interpretability: what triggers deeper planning?}

A key advantage of the discrete $K$ formulation is that we can directly analyse what
game features correlate with each planning depth.

\subsubsection{Pac-Man}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \todo{Figure: ghost distance by K (left), frightened-ghost fraction by K (right).}
  \caption{Context statistics for each $K$ choice in Pac-Man.
    \textbf{Left}: mean nearest-ghost distance.
    \textbf{Right}: fraction of steps in which at least one ghost is frightened.
    Error bars are 1 SE.}
  \label{fig:pacman_context}
\end{figure}

The clearest pattern in Pac-Man is ghost proximity (Figure~\ref{fig:pacman_context}).
When the nearest ghost is far away---the agent has room to maneuver---the policy
prefers $K{=}1$ and takes a quick, reactive action.
As the ghost closes in, the policy shifts to $K{=}2$ and (at extreme proximity) $K{=}4$,
investing more simulation depth to find safe escape routes.
Frightened ghosts reduce the stakes (the agent can safely eat them), and the policy
correspondingly reduces $K$, consistent with the interpretation that it is estimating
the value of additional computation rather than defaulting to a fixed depth.

\subsubsection{Real-time Tetris}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \todo{Figure: 3 panels --- board fill by K, stack height by K, mean K by piece type.}
  \caption{Context statistics for each $K$ choice in real-time Tetris.
    \textbf{Left}: board fill fraction (cells occupied / total cells).
    \textbf{Center}: stack height (rows with at least one filled cell).
    \textbf{Right}: mean $K$ per tetromino type.}
  \label{fig:tetris_context}

\paragraph{Board density.}
The most informative predictor of $K$ in Tetris is board fill (Figure~\ref{fig:tetris_context}, left).
$K{=}1$ is used almost exclusively on sparse boards (fill $\approx 0.133$, stack height
$\approx 6.8$ rows), where placement options are plentiful and any reasonable action
is acceptable.
$K{=}2$ and $K{=}3$ are used on significantly denser boards (fill $\approx 0.20$ and
$0.17$ respectively, stack heights $\approx 11$ and $\approx 9$ rows), where the choice
of column and rotation becomes critical and MCTS depth pays off.
$K{=}4$ is used sparingly (4.7\%) and coincides with full boards (fill $\approx 0.174$,
height $\approx 10.5$), potentially representing a last-resort deep search on the edge
of game-over.

\paragraph{Piece type.}
Different tetrominos elicit different mean planning depth
(Figure~\ref{fig:tetris_context}, right).
The I-piece receives the most deliberation ($\bar{K} = 2.39$): it is the only piece that
can clear four lines simultaneously, making optimal placement high-value, and its narrow
$1 \times 4$ footprint makes placement sensitive to the exact column and rotation.
The S-piece receives the least deliberation ($\bar{K} = 2.07$): its constrained geometry
means that placement options are limited regardless of how many simulations are run.

\paragraph{Piece vertical position.}
We also observe that higher $K$ choices tend to be made when the piece is lower on the
board ($K{=}1$: $\bar{y} = 3.31$; $K{=}3$: $\bar{y} = 6.85$).
Partly this is an artefact of the delay mechanism: choosing $K{=}3$ at $y{=}3$ means the
next decision occurs at $y{\approx}6$ by construction.
Nevertheless, the correlation with $K{=}1$ at the top of the board---where the piece has
just spawned and the agent has maximum time---is consistent with the board-fill finding.

In Pac-Man, $K{=}4$ at $\approx$9.9 FPS represents the slowest agent; the safe
real-time game speed is therefore approximately 9 FPS, at which $K{=}4$ MCTS finishes
exactly as 4 game frames elapse.

In Tetris RT, the effective FPS is nearly constant across all $K$ values (${\approx}42$--$53$
FPS).
This near-invariance arises because both the numerator (K env steps) and the denominator
(latency $\propto$ K simulations) scale linearly with $K$, canceling in the ratio.
The gating policy thus obtains ``free'' additional planning without sacrificing
throughput.

\section{Two-GPU Real-Time Deployment}
\label{sec:deployment}
% =============================================================================

The $K$-step framework maps directly onto a practical two-GPU real-time pipeline,
providing a proof of concept that our offline training translates to true asynchronous
deployment.

\paragraph{Architecture.}
GPU 0 hosts the game environment and the blueprint policy (a single AZNet forward pass
for each delay step, no MCTS).
GPU 1 hosts the MCTS tree search.
At each meta-decision, the current game state is transferred from GPU 0 to GPU 1;
GPU 1 begins MCTS and GPU 0 continues executing blueprint actions.
When GPU 1 finishes (after $K \times 32$ simulations), the selected action is applied
on GPU 0 at the next opportunity.

\paragraph{Timing calibration.}
At a game speed of 9 FPS, each game frame lasts $\approx$111ms.
$K{=}4$ MCTS (128 sims $\times$ $\approx$3ms per sim) completes in $\approx$384ms,
exactly matching 4 frames: the MCTS result is ready precisely when it should be applied.
$K{=}1$ and $K{=}2$ finish well within budget.
The gating policy determines $K$ before MCTS begins, based on the current game state,
so the pipeline is fully non-blocking.

\paragraph{Implication.}
This shows that the training protocol---which simulates the latency cost via no-op
actions in a batched simulator---accurately captures the dynamics of true real-time
execution.
No architectural changes are required to go from training to deployment.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{Value of computation.}
Our gating policy is, in essence, learning the value-of-computation function from
experience.
At each state it implicitly asks: ``Is the expected improvement from $K{=}3$ MCTS
over $K{=}2$ worth the two extra noop steps it requires?''
The answer varies by state---it is high when the board is dense and the placement decision
is consequential, and low when the board is sparse and any action is fine.
This is exactly the structure predicted by Russell and Wefald's~\cite{russell1991right}
theoretical framework, now realized in a neural gating policy trained end-to-end.

\paragraph{Implicit vs.\ explicit time pressure.}
The noop mechanism (Pac-Man, Tetris) makes the cost of computation implicit in game
outcomes; the clock mechanism (Speed Hex) makes it explicit and directly observable.
Both are learnable.
The noop formulation is easier to apply to any existing environment---add a no-op action
and define blueprint behavior---while the clock formulation provides a richer, more
interpretable cost signal.
We expect the two mechanisms to be largely interchangeable in practice.

\paragraph{Anytime planning.}
Our setting shares structure with anytime planning~\cite{dean1988analysis}: both improve
solution quality with more computation.
The key difference is the cost function.
In classical anytime planning, computation costs wall-clock time.
Here, computation costs \emph{game progression}---the environment advances during
thinking, and that progression has state-dependent consequences.
This makes the cost non-stationary and correlated with the value of the computation
itself, a richer problem structure that SMDP-based learning is well-suited to address.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The gating policy is trained on top of a \emph{fixed} base model; jointly training the
base and gating policy is an open problem.
We study the discrete set $K \in \Kset$; continuous compute allocation is a natural
extension.
Our environments are simulation-based; extending to physical real-time settings
(robotics, real-time strategy) requires dealing with stochastic and uncontrolled
latencies.


% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

We introduced the concept of implicit time pressure in reinforcement learning---the
regime where computation time has a real cost paid in game outcomes, not merely in
wall-clock seconds.
We showed that AlphaZero-style MCTS scales approximately linearly with compute in
real-time environments, motivating adaptive compute allocation as the central design
choice.
Our lightweight gating policy, trained via PPO over a frozen AlphaZero base, learns to
allocate computation proportionally to state complexity: quick reactive decisions on easy
states and deep planning on critical ones.
Across Pac-Man, real-time Tetris, and speed Hex, the gating policy outperforms every
fixed-budget baseline while using less average compute, and its strategy is directly
interpretable in terms of game-theoretic features.
We believe adaptive computation under real-time pressure is a natural and underexplored
axis for making RL systems both more capable and more resource-efficient.

\section{Environment Details}

\subsection{Real-time Tetris}

The real-time Tetris environment is built on the Jumanji Tetris base~\cite{jumanji2022}.
Every call to \texttt{env.step(action)} advances the falling piece by one gravity row
regardless of the action taken.
The action set is: \{left, right, rotate-CW, rotate-CCW, hard-drop, noop\} (indices 0--5).
Action 5 (noop) applies no horizontal or rotational input but gravity still acts,
modeling the piece falling while the agent deliberates.
A piece locks when gravity would move it into an occupied cell or below the board floor.
On lock, completed rows are cleared and a new piece spawns at the top.
The episode terminates when a new piece cannot be placed without overlap (board topped out)
or after 2000 gravity ticks.

\subsection{K-step wrapping}

The $K$-step execution wrapper is implemented as a subclass of the base environment.
For a chosen depth $K$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Run MCTS with $K \times 32$ simulations from the current state.
  \item Execute $K-1$ blueprint or no-op actions in the real environment.
  \item Execute the MCTS-selected action as the $K$-th step.
  \item Accumulate rewards from all $K$ steps.
\end{enumerate}
For Tetris, steps 2 uses action=5 (noop) so gravity acts naturally.
For Pac-Man, steps 2 uses the argmax of AZNet policy logits (blueprint).
The MCTS tree is initialized fresh at each meta-decision.

\section{Gating Network Details}

\subsection{Architecture}

The residual block used in the spatial branch is ResBlockV2 with Layer Normalization:
pre-activation design (LN $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Conv $\to$ LN $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Conv) with a
skip connection.
All convolutions are $3\times3$ with 64 output channels and SAME padding.

The time embedding projects the normalized step count $\hat{t} = t / T_{\max}$ through:
$\mathbb{R}^2 \to$ Linear(32) $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Linear(64).
The input is $(\hat{t}, 1 - \hat{t})$ (time elapsed and time remaining).

\subsection{Training stability}

We found that entropy regularization is essential to prevent premature collapse onto a
single $K$ value.
Without entropy bonus, the policy quickly converges to always-$K{=}3$ (the best fixed
baseline) and stops exploring.
With entropy coef.\ = 0.01, the policy continues to explore and eventually surpasses the
always-$K{=}3$ baseline.
